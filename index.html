02research

  1. MIT CSAIL

    Researcher, AI for Science & Mathematics

    nov 2026 — now

    I work on AI for math: getting machine learning models to write proofs that can actually be checked. A lot of that is formalization, which means rewriting a math problem in a proof assistant so a computer can verify every step.

    From there, I look at how models can search for proofs in that setting. It’s a nice place to do ML research because the answer is never fuzzy: the proof either checks or it doesn’t.

  2. Independent

    Open problems in mathematics, with Claude

    now

    In my free time I work on open math problems with Claude. I use it to try small cases, look for patterns or counterexamples, and talk through ideas for a proof, then go back and check the arguments carefully myself.

    Most of these problems won’t be solved in one sitting, so I keep track of partial progress too: special cases that work out, simpler versions of the problem, and approaches that didn’t pan out.

  3. University of Toronto

    Research Assistant, Harmonic Analysis · Dept. of Mathematics

    jun — sep 2026

    Research in harmonic analysis on maximal averages: operators that take, at each point, the largest average of a function over a whole family of shapes, such as spheres of every radius. The central question is for which exponents p these operators stay bounded on Lp. I investigated those bounds with Littlewood–Paley theory, which splits a function into pieces at different frequency scales, and Calderón–Zygmund theory, which controls the singular parts that are left over.

    Alongside that, I surveyed 40+ papers on Fourier restriction, a closely related problem about how the Fourier transform of a function behaves on curved surfaces like spheres and paraboloids. To ground the theory, I wrote NumPy checks of conjectured decay rates for oscillatory integrals, testing numerically whether the predicted rates actually held.

  4. STEMinate

    Research Intern, Brain–Computer Interfaces

    apr — aug 2025

    Sole author of Dream Engineering, guided by researchers from Neuralink and the University of Michigan. The paper reviews how brain–computer interfaces, neurostimulation and sensory cues can detect, influence and record dreams, drawing on 50 sources across sleep neuroscience, neural engineering, clinical psychology and neuroethics. It evaluates the main techniques available today, from decoding sleep imagery and replaying sound cues to closed-loop stimulation and real-time communication with lucid dreamers, along with their uses in nightmare treatment, skill learning and creativity.

    Because shaping what someone experiences while asleep raises hard questions, the paper also examines consent, mental privacy and the risks of frequent lucid-dream induction, alongside recent neural-data laws and UNESCO’s neurotechnology ethics guidance. It closes by proposing a consent-based, closed-loop system design and a pilot study to test it.

    paper Dream Engineering doi:10.5281/zenodo.24373952 ↗
